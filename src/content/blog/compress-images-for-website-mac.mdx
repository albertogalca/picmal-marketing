Last week I ran Google PageSpeed on a client's site and got a 34 on mobile. The biggest problem? Images. A single hero image was 4.2 MB. Product photos were 3000px wide, served as uncompressed PNGs. The page took 11 seconds to load on a decent connection.

This happens more often than you'd think. You export from Figma or Lightroom, upload directly to your CMS, and everything looks fine on your fast office Wi-Fi. Then real users on real connections get a loading spinner for eight seconds and leave.

The fix is straightforward, but there are three things that actually matter — and a lot of advice online that doesn't.

## What actually matters for web images

### 1. Format choice

This is the biggest lever. A JPG hero image at 1400px wide might be 800 KB. The same image as WebP is around 300 KB. As AVIF, closer to 200 KB.

WebP has near-universal browser support now — over 97% globally. AVIF is at about 93% and climbing. Unless you're targeting very old browsers, there's no reason to serve JPG or PNG on the web anymore.

PNG still makes sense for images that need transparency and sharp edges (logos, icons, screenshots with text). But for photos? WebP or AVIF, every time.

### 2. Dimensions

Nobody needs a 4000px wide image on a website. Most sites have a content area of 800-1200px. Even full-width hero images rarely need to be wider than 1600px on a standard display, or 2400px if you're serving retina.

Resizing from 4000px to 1600px alone can cut file size by 75%, before you even touch compression. This is the step most people skip.

### 3. Compression quality

Here's the thing most guides get wrong: they tell you to find the "perfect" quality setting. In practice, 80% quality is visually indistinguishable from 100% for photos on the web. At screen resolution, viewed in a browser, side by side — you cannot tell the difference.

Below 70% you start to see artifacts, especially in gradients and areas with subtle color transitions. So the sweet spot is 75-85%. I default to 80% and only go higher for images where fine detail actually matters (portfolio sites, product photography).

## Free alternatives (honest takes)

There are good free options. Whether they're good enough depends on your volume and which formats you need.

### macOS Preview

Built into every Mac. Open an image, File > Export, choose JPEG, drag the quality slider down. It works.

The problems: no WebP or AVIF export. No batch processing unless you select multiple files in the sidebar, and even then it's clunky. No resize option during export. Fine for a one-off, painful for regular use.

### Squoosh (squoosh.app)

Google's web-based compression tool. Genuinely good — it shows you a before/after comparison and supports WebP and AVIF. The compression algorithms are solid.

The catch: one image at a time. If you have 50 product photos to compress, you'll be clicking and dragging for an hour. It also requires uploading your images to a web page, though processing happens client-side in the browser.

### ImageOptim

A well-made Mac app that's been around for years. Drag files in, it compresses them in place. Good for lossless and lossy optimization of JPG and PNG files.

The limitation: it optimizes existing formats but doesn't convert to WebP or AVIF. So you're getting smaller JPGs, not the 60-70% size reduction you'd get from switching formats entirely.

### TinyPNG / TinyJPG

Web-based, works well, supports WebP now. Free tier limits you to 20 images at a time with a 5 MB per-file limit.

The trade-off: your images get uploaded to their servers. For personal projects, probably fine. For client work with unreleased product photos or confidential material, that's a different conversation.

## The workflow that actually works

After compressing images for dozens of sites, this is what I've settled on:

1. **Resize to max display width.** Figure out the widest your image will ever appear on the site. Multiply by 2 for retina. That's your max width. For most sites: 1600px for hero images, 1200px for content images, 800px for thumbnails.

2. **Convert to WebP.** If you need to support very old browsers, keep a JPG fallback. Most sites don't need to anymore.

3. **Compress to 80% quality.** Don't overthink this. 80% is the right default for 95% of web images.

4. **Check your results.** After compression, a typical 1600px-wide photo should land between 100-300 KB as WebP. If it's over 500 KB, something's off — usually the dimensions are still too large.

## Why compressing locally matters

If you're building sites for clients, you're handling their product photos, unreleased designs, internal documentation screenshots, maybe photos of prototypes that haven't been announced yet.

Uploading those to TinyPNG or any web-based tool means they hit someone else's server. Even if the service says they delete files after processing, you're still transmitting them. For some clients, that's a non-starter. For regulated industries, it can be a compliance issue.

Processing everything locally on your Mac means the files never leave your machine. There's no terms of service to read, no data processing agreement to sign, no question about where the images end up. They stay on your disk, compressed, ready to use.

This isn't theoretical paranoia. I've had clients specifically ask whether their images were being processed through any third-party services. Being able to say "everything happens locally, offline" is a real advantage.
